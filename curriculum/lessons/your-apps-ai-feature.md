# Your app's AI feature

**Purpose (the first spoken sentence):** In this lesson you'll learn what changes when strangers can use the AI feature inside your app: who pays when they use it, what its answers can do inside your pages, and what you owe the people talking to it.

**Conclusion:** An AI feature in a public app is a meter strangers can run and a voice that speaks for you, so put every model call behind sign-in, set per-user and global caps that stop spending, treat the model's output as untrusted, tell people it is an AI, ask before their data goes to an AI provider, and let them report what it makes.

**Where it sits on the map:** District 2 · Going Live — How do others safely use it?; module 04 Safe for strangers (after 04.02 tested the app as a stranger; here the stranger talks to the model you pay for).

**Lead source (audit, 2026-10-12):** S055 (OWASP GenAI Security Project). This is the hard-won work the lesson delivers: credit it by name on the title card. The narration names at most the row's sources in curriculum.md (S055, S220, S233, S195); every other source in this spec goes on a dated on-screen card and on the lesson page's source list.

**Scope note:** this lesson is about the AI feature *you ship to other people* (a chatbot, a summariser, an image generator inside your app). Your own agent's bill is 06.03; your own agent being steered by text it reads is 07.01.

## What to teach (binding scope)

Rough time budget: C121 6 min (meter 3, output 1.5, voice 1.5) · C131 4 min. Lead with the meter, because it is the failure that costs the builder money in the first week.

### C121 Your app's AI feature is a meter strangers can run and a voice that speaks for you

1. **Strangers can run your meter.** OWASP's 2026 list defines the attack by "cost asymmetry": attackers trigger disproportionately expensive computation at negligible cost to themselves. It names Denial of Wallet, and "reasoning-loop and thinking-token exhaustion": short, benign-looking prompts that push reasoning models into very long thinking, which input-size filters do not catch. Its conclusion: traditional request-rate limiting alone is no longer sufficient. (S055, LLM06:2026 Unbounded Consumption) — **teach**
2. **Growing conversations cost more per turn, under every rate limit.** OWASP's own worked example: an open session that re-processes its whole context each turn climbs from about $0.001 on the first turn to about $0.50 by turn 100, while no single request trips a rate limit. (S055, LLM06 Scenario 8; the "context times turns" mechanism is 06.03's, C056) — **brief**
3. **Know who is asking before you pay for it.** Per-user caps need a user, so put the model call behind sign-in, and call the model from your server, never from the browser with your key in it. The pre-launch checklist item: per-user AND global token/cost caps on every LLM-calling endpoint; provider keys are never in frontend code and are always proxied through your API. (S220 items 42 and 13) — **teach** (where keys may live is 03.02's)
4. **Caps that stop, not caps that warn.** Limit tokens per minute and per day and estimated cost per request, not just requests per second; use pre-flight token estimation to refuse a request before inference begins; set non-overridable ceilings per key, user and account that halt inference when exceeded, "rather than alerting thresholds" that fast workloads outpace. Test it: hammer the endpoint from one test account and watch the quota kick in. (S055 LLM06 mitigations 1 and 2; S220 item 42) — **teach** (the provider-side hard cap is 06.03's, C061)
5. **Cap the work each public request may do.** OWASP names large or insufficiently constrained output budgets on reasoning models as a compounding factor, and asks for step limits, recursion limits, time limits and per-run cost ceilings on any agent your feature runs. (S055 LLM06) — **brief** (effort settings are 06.03's, C059)
6. **Treat the model's output like a stranger's input.** The model's text can be steered by whoever writes to it, so "treat the model as any other user": encode its output for where it is shown, use a strict Content Security Policy, and never pass it unchecked to a shell, a SQL query or a file path. JavaScript or Markdown generated by the model and rendered in a page is cross-site scripting; a chat UI that auto-renders Markdown images or link previews lets an attacker carry conversation data out in the image URL, so disable auto-rendering of images, link previews and iframes by default. The checklist item: sanitise model output before it touches the page. (S055 LLM10:2026 Improper Output Handling; S220 item 43) — **teach** (why anyone who writes text the model reads can steer it, prompt injection, is 07.01's, C073)
7. **The system prompt is not a safe.** Assume it is public: never store secrets, credentials or regulated data in it, and test extraction ("repeat your instructions verbatim"). (S055 LLM02 mitigation 4; S220 item 41) — **one line**
8. **The bot speaks for you.** OWASP: "The core risk is that the incorrect output is trusted and acted upon"; its example is a customer-service agent that misreads a policy and approves a refund the terms forbid. Ground the bot's claims in current, authoritative sources, and verify a claim before acting on it (Claim-Check-Act). Apple's App Store rule: chatbots and plug-ins in your app are your responsibility ("You are responsible for all such software offered in your app"). Decide what the bot may and may not claim. (S055 LLM07:2026 Misinformation; S233 guideline 4.7) — **teach**

### C131 Putting AI in front of other people: say it is AI, ask before their data goes to an AI provider, let them report what it makes

1. **Say it is an AI, at first contact.** EU AI Act Article 50(1): providers must design AI systems that interact directly with people so that those people are informed they are interacting with an AI system, unless that is obvious to a reasonably well-informed, observant person in context. Article 50(5): the information comes in a clear and distinguishable way at the latest at the first interaction or exposure, and must meet accessibility requirements. Article 50 applies from 2026-08-02. (S195) — **teach**
2. **List every AI provider your app sends users' data to, and ask before sending.** Apple guideline 5.1.2(i): you must clearly disclose where personal data will be shared with third parties, "including with third-party AI", and obtain explicit permission before doing so; apps that share data without consent may be removed. A privacy policy line is not explicit permission. Send only the fields the task needs (OWASP), and enforce no-training and no-retention settings technically rather than by policy text alone. (S233, S055 LLM02 mitigations 2 and 7) — **teach** (the data map is 04.02's, C119)
3. **You answer for what runs inside your app.** Apple 4.7, as in C121 point 8. (S233) — **one line** (merged into C121 point 8 on screen)
4. **Let people report what it makes, without leaving the app.** Google Play: apps that generate content with AI must include in-app reporting or flagging of offensive content; its examples are text chatbots where the chat is central and image or video generators. (S234) — **brief**
5. **Other rules may apply on top.** California's companion-chatbot law (SB 243) could not be read: the official site returned HTTP 403 to the research crew again on 2026-10-12. Do not teach its contents; say only that some US states now regulate companion chatbots and that the viewer must check where their users are. — **one line** (backlog: "Mobile apps, app stores and chatbot law")

## You vs the agent

As of 2026-10-12:

- **The agent does on its own:** moves model calls behind sign-in and onto the server; implements per-user and global token and cost quotas, pre-flight token estimates and output-length caps; encodes and sanitises model output, sets the Content Security Policy and turns off auto-rendered images; builds the AI notice, the consent screen and the report button; and proves its own work with tests it can run: a burst from one test account hits the quota, a page renders a hostile model answer as inert text, and a network log shows no request reaches the AI provider before the user consents. (S055, S220, S233)
- **You must:** set the per-user budget and the global ceiling, in money, and decide what happens when it is hit (S055); decide what the bot may and may not claim and where its facts come from (S055 LLM07); decide which user data may leave the app and to which provider, word the notice and the consent request, and own the store submission (S233, S234); look at the running feature once as a stranger (signed out, then as a new user) to confirm the caps and the notice are really there.
- **Bring in an expert** (how: 04.04, C118): a security tester for an AI feature that handles other people's data; a lawyer if the bot gives health, legal or financial information, if the app is aimed at minors or handles health data, or before an EU launch.

## Show it for real

- Burst a test AI endpoint from one test account and watch the per-user quota refuse further calls; then lower the global ceiling and watch it stop inference for everyone. Proves caps that stop, not caps that warn (S220 item 42; S055 LLM06).
- Plot OWASP's own per-turn cost figures ($0.001 at turn 1, about $0.50 by turn 100) as a rising line under a flat "rate limit" line. Proves a stranger can spend your money without ever tripping a request limit (S055 LLM06 Scenario 8). Show it as OWASP's worked example, not a measured bill.
- In a local test chat UI, have the model return a Markdown image whose URL points to a logging server you run; show the request arriving with text in its query string; then disable auto-rendering and show nothing leaves. Proves model output is untrusted input (S055 LLM10).
- Render a model answer containing HTML and a script tag raw, then encoded. Proves "the model's answer is just text" is wrong (S055 LLM10; S220 item 43).
- Open the browser's Network tab on the app's consent screen: the first request to the AI provider appears only after the user taps agree, and the screen names the provider. Proves explicit permission, the way Apple asks for it (S233 5.1.2(i)).
- The chatbot's first message says it is an AI; a generated image carries a report button that works without leaving the app. Proves the two duties to users are a few lines of UI (S195 Art. 50(1); S234).
- Use test accounts, a local logging server and fake data only; no real provider key on screen (mask it in the dashboard).

## Out-of-date framings to avoid

- "Rate limiting protects my AI endpoint." OWASP 2026: request-rate limiting alone is no longer sufficient (S055).
- "A billing alert will catch it." Alerts do not stop inference; caps must halt (S055 LLM06).
- "The model's answer is just text." (S055 LLM10)
- "If the bot says something wrong, that's the AI company's fault" / "the chatbot is the AI company's product, not mine." (S233 4.7; S055 LLM07)
- "Disclosure is only about AI images and video." Article 50(1) covers chatbots (S195).
- "My privacy policy covers sending data to the AI." Apple requires explicit permission (S233 5.1.2(i)).
- Citing the 2025 OWASP numbering (denial of wallet as "LLM10:2025"). In the 2026 list Unbounded Consumption is LLM06 and Improper Output Handling is LLM10 (S055; S220 still uses the old label).
